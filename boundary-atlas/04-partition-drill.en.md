# The Boundary Atlas · 04 — The drill, and the contract this series kept deferring

*A design study in five figures. Vendor-neutral, model-neutral, deliberately generic — the point is a way of thinking, not a reference implementation. No client data appears anywhere in this series.*

**An architecture is what it does when the link is down.** On the first page, invariant 2 declared partition a premise rather than an outage. That sentence was easy to write; this is where it gets tested. So: cut the federation interface between two regions and watch what the design actually yields — at the cut, a day in, a week in, and at reconnect. Then write the evidence contract this series has been handing forward since figure 0, and check honestly which reservations it lets us close.

**The drill has a precondition, and it matters.** FP-1's max-ages run from each artifact's own freshness, not from the moment the link drops — and re-fetching an artifact does not reset that clock. A policy snapshot issued six days before the cut expires a day into the partition, not seven, however recently it was pulled. So the drill runs from a clean baseline — every peer artifact newly issued and holding its full budget at the cut — and the timeline below is the best case, not the general one. In production the order of expiry is not fixed and a class can lapse at T0 itself, which is why each cell records the source freshness timestamp, the age, and the remaining budget of every artifact it is relying on, alongside the partition state. And the boundary is inclusive: an artifact at exactly 24 hours is still within a 24-hour max-age. Expiry happens past the limit, not at it.

**T0 — almost nothing happens, and the exception is the honest part.** The link drops. No ceremony fails because of the cut, no checkpoint queues, no operator is paged at 3 a.m. Each cell keeps verifying against its own last-known-good snapshot, because figure 3's trust registry made that snapshot a local asset rather than a cache of something remote. Locally sourced anchors, local status data, and locally evaluated BL-1 verdicts keep refreshing straight through the cut; only peer-sourced entries and ledger-authority policy freeze. The decision ceiling is unchanged.

The exception: a credential revoked by a peer issuer one minute after the cut is invisible here for the remainder of that artifact's status window. The *base* ceiling is unchanged, and a tier that accepts the remaining window keeps deciding at full assurance — which is a residual, not an oversight, and it belongs to the tier that accepts it. It is also the reason partition state is a decision input rather than a log field: a tier may override the base ceiling the moment the cell knows it is partitioned, tightening its own status max-age or refusing outright rather than spending a window it can no longer justify. The two are separate settings, and both are recorded.

**A day in — the first thing to go stale is the thing that matters most.** Peer status data ages past its FP-1 max-age of 24 hours, and revocation is precisely what a partition cannot refresh across the border. Checks that depend on peer revocation lose their freshness basis and drop to indeterminate; each risk tier then resolves indeterminate in its own way, which is the point of having tiers at all. A parcel gate may pass; a controlled-goods release refuses. Nobody is pretending a 30-hour-old peer revocation list is current, and nobody is stopping the whole supply chain because it isn't.

**A week in — one control switches itself off.** Peer anchors and policy age past their own FP-1 max-age, and unknown or expired anchors resolve to indeterminate. Peer-issued BL-1 verdicts expire too — they are signed, versioned, and expiring by design, and they ride as policy cargo — so the per-use path disables itself wherever it depended on a peer's verdict. Falling back to the derived path takes two conditions, not one: a qualified derived binding must be installed, *and* the anchors, status data, and policy that path depends on must themselves be within FP-1 — which, a week into a partition, means locally sourced. A qualified binding does not repair an expired anchor. Where either condition fails, the honest outcome is indeterminate and no allow.

One correction to a tempting story: this does not close BL-1's privacy residual. That channel is issuer-visible refill activity, and BL-1's contract deliberately makes it independent of verification — fixed cadence, fixed quantity, cover events even in windows where nobody verified anything. A partition idles the verifier side; the refill schedule keeps running exactly as published, which is what makes it uninformative in the first place. BL-1 stays on the residual list at the end of this piece.

**Reconnect — epochs before traffic.** The link returns and nothing resumes on the strength of connectivity alone. The two trust registries compare ledger epochs and content hashes first. Same epoch and hash: conditional cargo resumes, status data catches up, and stale-window indeterminates clear as the snapshots refresh. Different epochs: the intersection of the two allowlists with conditions conjoined defines the effective ceiling, but conditional cargo stays blocked until both ends confirm the same epoch — so the only traffic on the strip is figure 1's staged epoch transition itself, after which ordinary cargo resumes on the new epoch. Same epoch with a different hash is not a tie to break: it is an equivocating ledger, and republishing that epoch would be indistinguishable from the equivocation it is meant to repair. Recovery is an increment — the authority publishes the next epoch through the same two-stage path, both registries record the equivocating epoch as revoked, and the conflict and its resolution are written as regional evidence on each side. That works only while the two ends still share something to validate against: the increment is accepted if both can verify it under the last authority and schema they mutually accepted before the split. If the equivocation reached the authority keys or the schema themselves, there is no common basis left, and no clever protocol invents one — conditional cargo stays blocked until the registries are re-provisioned out-of-band by the ledger approval authority with the receiving trust registry operators, and that re-provisioning leaves its own regional evidence on each side. Nothing is backfilled across the border: evidence never crossed it and does not start now.

**The evidence contract.** Every figure since 0 has handed this forward. Here it is, with numbers, because a contract without numbers is a preference.

*Classes, retention, readers.* Attempt evidence and the failure journal are kept two years, read by that verifier domain's operators and auditors through that domain's partition. Partition-access records — every refused, failed, or break-glass attempt to reach across a partition — are kept five years, and they are written **where the failure was actually observed**: a request refused at the source gate, or lost in transport before it arrived, never executes a line of code in the target, so it is recorded in the source domain and read by that domain's operators and auditors; a request that reached the target gate and was denied there, or admitted under break-glass, is recorded in the target domain and read by that domain's. The two are never forwarded to each other and never joined — moving a record across to tidy the filing would break the very prohibition it exists to evidence. Observation data under rule 9 is kept thirty days, purpose-limited, read only within its own verifier domain. Refill records are kept three years in the issuing region, read by the Issuer operator and that region's auditors, and by no verifier domain, peer region, or federation aggregate. Health records are kept one year and drill results three, both read by regional operators and auditors. The schedule is published per cell by the regional platform operator, so jurisdictions can differ, and amending it runs the same four approval gates as the ledger.

*Closure custody.* A cell that closes six months after an attempt still owes that record eighteen more months, and the operator who wrote it is leaving. So before a closure completes, every unexpired class transfers to a named successor evidence custodian in the same jurisdiction, and the transfer is acknowledged; without that acknowledgement the closure is not complete and the region does not stop. The custodian inherits the per-domain reader boundaries, the keys, and the administration, deletes each class at its own expiry, and retains the disposal attestation for ten years. The jurisdiction's regulator is a reader of that attestation, not the custodian of the evidence — an ambiguity worth removing, because a regulator who is quietly the custodian is a federation aggregate with a different name.

*Fields.* An attempt record carries the outcome, the attempt identifier, the unit state, the enforcement effect status including its failures, the decision timestamp, the partition state, and the failure stage where there was one. It also carries what makes the decision reconstructible without identifying anyone: the non-identifying predicate or control identifier evaluated, the transaction intent and risk tier, the taxonomy leaf and binding used, the trust, status, and binding validation results with a structured reason or failure code, and the coarse result with its retry condition — plus the source freshness, age, and remaining budget of each artifact relied on. Version strings alone would not survive the retention period, so every signed policy, snapshot, and binding manifest that a decision referenced is itself kept for those two years as an immutable, hash-resolvable archive inside the same domain's reader boundary. A version number that points at something already overwritten explains nothing. The failure journal carries less by design: attempt identifier, stage, timestamp, and what could not be written. A partition-access record carries the principal, the source and target partitions, the decision, the timestamp, and the break-glass authorization reference where there is one.

*Observation data gets its own line*, since rule 9 was deferred here twice. Its fields are the checkpoint time, the location, and the transport attributes actually observed — with no stable subject key, enforced at write time rather than at query time, and no join path across verifier domains. Thirty days, that domain's operators and auditors, and nothing else.

*What is never recorded*: raw proofs, disclosed claim values, and stable holder or credential identifiers.

*And if it cannot be written*: no allow. The attempt resolves to indeterminate, the independent journal — per verifier domain, on its own sink — records that it could not be evidenced, and a health record says so where that domain's operators and auditors can see it.

**Reservations: what actually closes.** Being precise here matters more than sounding finished. SU-1's closure test is now *executable* end to end — first use, controlled retry, replay and reuse rejection, unknown state yielding no allow, and evidence both reaching its authorized readers and being unreachable from any other domain, peer region, or federation aggregate. Executable is not passed. The regional verifier operator runs the full five-part test before a domain serves live traffic, and re-runs it after any change to the commit path, the unit classifier, enforcement or effect-status handling, evidence projection, the failure journal, or a reader or partition boundary — because each of those can break a leg of the test without touching the others. Until a pass record exists, the domain enables no presentation-capable leaf at all: rule 10 governs every path that reaches a verifier, so gating only the per-use path would leave the unlinkable one live on an unproven commit. Only on a current pass record can ledger rule 10 be re-verified here as SU-1's closure gate, instead of pointing forward to a figure that had not been written yet. BL-1's refill records are retained under the schedule above — together with the signed schedule manifest, each window's acceptance result, and the verdict lineage, since a two-way match cannot be re-run three years later against records whose schedule has been forgotten — and an operationally expired verdict is never re-activated; it is re-earned.

The residuals that stay open are the ones this series declared out loud: cross-domain reuse on the per-use path, whose prevention rests on the holder's wallet rather than on anything a verifier enforces; the bounded blind window after a cut, during which a peer's fresh revocation is unseen and the accepting risk tier owns the exposure — partition-aware tightening shortens that window but does not remove it; the correlation channels outside the credential layer, each with an owner in the ledger; BL-1's issuer-visible refill cadence; the bootstrap that must arrive out-of-band because no chain can validate its own first link; the ledger approval authority, whose unavailability freezes epochs while cells keep running; any binding admitted under an explicitly reduced promise; and break-glass access, per-partition and audited. Business data remains governed by a policy that is not this one.

**Run it, don't admire it.** A partition drill is a rehearsal with a cadence, not a diagram: quarterly, and after every ledger epoch change, owned by the regional platform operator, with the clock actually advanced past both max-ages rather than simulated at the whiteboard. A drill fails if any decision exceeded its stated ceiling, if any attempt was allowed without evidence, or if reconnect resumed conditional cargo before epoch confirmation; a drill that cannot be run at all is a failure too. A failed drill blocks the next epoch activation in that cell until a *full-scope re-run passes* — re-running it and failing again lifts nothing. The result is a coarse artifact, signed, versioned, and expiring — window, pass or fail, evidence digest, issuing authority — and it travels as policy the way the BL-1 verdict does, validated locally by the receiving trust registry. Raw drill evidence stays in each region's own partitions. The regional platform operator runs the drill; the trust registry operator enforces the gate, because the party that would most like to skip a failing drill should not be the party holding the release switch. A design that has never been cut apart on purpose is a design whose failure behavior is a hypothesis.

**Five figures, one habit.** Borders before arrows, and cargo before flows. A ledger someone can sign. A ceremony whose most important arrow is the one that isn't drawn. A deployment that names the single layer where products are allowed to matter. And a drill that treats degradation as a designed outcome rather than an incident. Outside that one layer, nothing here needed a product name to carry a load — and that, rather than any particular scheme, is the part worth taking to the next system.
