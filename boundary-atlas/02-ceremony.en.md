# The Boundary Atlas · 02 — The ceremony, and the second missing arrow

*A design study in five figures. Vendor-neutral, model-neutral, deliberately generic — the point is a way of thinking, not a reference implementation. No client data appears anywhere in this series.*

**Figures 0 and 1 froze space; figure 2 is time.** The boundary map said what may cross; the ledger made it enumerable and signable. A ceremony is where those promises either hold or quietly leak — a sequence of small steps, and the privacy of the whole is decided as much by the arrows that never appear as by the ones that do.

**Walking the lifelines.** The holder arrives with credentials in custody. Which region issued each one is context the ledger evaluates, not a field this ceremony adds — and no presentation cargo or underlying attribute has crossed to the verifier yet.

1. **The challenge comes first, and it is not a greeting.** It is fresh, verifier-issued, and bound to this verifier domain and this transaction intent. It is also the attempt identifier: every step below is keyed to it, independently of which artifact the holder ends up presenting.
2. **Derivation is holder-side.** The wallet derives a qualified unlinkable presentation on the device, binding it to the challenge, the domain, and the intent with the gate's qualified mechanism — or selects a qualified per-use credential from its batch, whose answer to that challenge carries the same binding. Either way, nothing has yet crossed to the verifier.
3. **The presentation crosses — an attestation; no other declared cargo.** At the same instant, observation happens: the checkpoint sees transport metadata nobody declared as cargo. Rule 9 owns that moment — regional-only, per-verifier, never joined.
4. **Binding is checked before policy.** The verifier first verifies that this presentation is bound to its own fresh challenge, its domain, and this transaction intent — and that the cryptographic proof verifies. A wrong-domain or recorded presentation is rejected here. Rejection is not silence: it becomes a deny or indeterminate on the regional attempt record, under the same evidence rules as a success. Only then does the decision point consult the last-known-good snapshot — trust anchors, status data, policy, under FP-1 max-ages. Note what is absent: no live dependency, no question asked outside the region at decision time.
5. **The candidate decision is computed — not yet enforced.** Three-valued: allow, deny, indeterminate. Each risk tier resolves indeterminate in its own way, except the SU-1 case, which no tier may promote to allow.
6. **One atomic commit, keyed to the attempt.** The key is the attempt identifier from step 1 — not the artifact — and one attempt binds exactly one unit commitment: the same attempt arriving with a different unit is a conflict, and a conflict never allows. The unit itself is defined per taxonomy leaf: on the batch path, the per-use credential instance; on the derived path, the challenge-bound presentation transcript. A single atomic commit binds the attempt identifier, the unit state, the immutable outcome, and the regional evidence record — before anything is enforced or returned. If that commit or its evidence cannot be confirmed, the enforcement point must not allow; if the evidence store itself is uncertain, a separate failure journal records that the attempt could not be evidenced, readable by the same regional operators and auditors, and the answer is still no allow.
7. **Enforcement is idempotent, and confirmed before it is claimed.** The effect is an attempt-keyed operation whose status is tracked durably: a final allow is returned only after the effect is confirmed, an unknown effect resolves to indeterminate and never allows, and a retry resumes the recorded state instead of creating a second effect. The effect's status, including its failures, is written to the same regional record before any final result leaves.
8. **The result returns coarse** — with retry conditions, correlator-free: enough for the holder to act on, nothing for two checkpoints to join. The evidence contract goes to figure 4, which owns schema, retention, and readers for everything step 6 and step 7 wrote.

**What SU-1 does not close.** SU-1 is at-most-once *within a verifier domain* — that is what a verifier can enforce from its own state. Across domains the two paths differ, and the honest answer is uncomfortable on one of them. On the derived path there is no gap: a presentation is bound to one domain's challenge, so a second domain rejects it on binding alone, locally, with nothing shared. On the per-use path there is a gap: a legitimate holder, keys entirely under their own control, can answer two domains' fresh challenges with the same instance, and each domain sees a first use. Closing that would mean two domains comparing notes about one credential — precisely the cross-domain correlator invariant 5 forbids. So the design refuses the correlator and declares the residual instead: cross-domain single use on the per-use path rests on the holder's wallet marking an instance spent in its secure element. That is an assumption about the holder's device, not a guarantee the verifier enforces, and a modified wallet defeats it undetectably. A deployment that cannot accept that assumption does not adopt the per-use path — it uses the derived path, where binding closes the gap by construction.

**The second missing arrow.** Figure 0's first missing arrow kept raw PII and source documents out of the checkpoint, the verifier, and the federation lane. This one keeps the *fact of verification* away from the issuer: the verifier sends no direct per-ceremony callback or event — not for status, not for confirmation, not "just for telemetry." A callback would quietly make the issuer a rendezvous point, a single desk where who-verified-whom-and-when accumulates without anyone ever deciding it should. It would also chain the ceremony to the issuer's uptime and to every link between here and there; this ceremony depends only on a snapshot it already holds. One honest caveat, in the spirit of figure 0's scope-and-limits: on the per-use path, batches are finite, and refills are visible to the issuer in aggregate. That residual is accepted and controlled under a named reservation, BL-1 — owner: the Issuer operator in the issuing region; target: figure 3's issuer plane. Its contract: refill cadence and quantity are fixed in advance and independent of consumption, with demand-driven emergency refills prohibited; a stockout falls back to the derived path only where a qualified derived capability exists, and otherwise resolves to indeterminate and no allow; refill evidence stays with the issuing region's auditors. Its acceptance test is mechanical: auditors compare issuer-visible refill cadence and quantity across different ceremony traces, including intervals with zero use, and BL-1 passes only if they are indistinguishable — for as long as it fails, the per-use path is disabled.

**What the verifier is left holding.** One approved predicate presentation — a boolean or a proof. A spent-unit marker meaningful only inside this domain. An observation record under rule 9, regional-only and never joined — that channel is constrained, not denied. What it never held, even for a second: an underlying attribute, a source document, or a stable holder or credential correlator in the presentation transcript or status evidence.

**Open by design.** SU-1 is pinned down here at the interface level; its durable-evidence half is defined in figure 4, and when that happens, rule 10 of the ledger — SU-1's closure gate — is re-verified. A design study that pretended it had no open ends would be — the phrase is familiar by now — lying with a diagram.

**Next.** Figure 3 stamps the standard cell across regions: deployment, the adapter layer, and the only place vendors are allowed to exist. Figure 4 rehearses the partition and writes the evidence contract this figure just promised.
