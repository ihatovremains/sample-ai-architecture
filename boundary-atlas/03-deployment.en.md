# The Boundary Atlas · 03 — The standard cell, and where products are allowed to live

*A design study in five figures. Vendor-neutral, model-neutral, deliberately generic — the point is a way of thinking, not a reference implementation. No client data appears anywhere in this series.*

**Deployment is where neutrality stops being a slogan.** Figures 0 through 2 have been careful never to let a product name carry a load: roles, cargo, rules, and one ceremony have carried it instead. But something eventually has to be bought, installed, and patched. Figure 3 says where — one layer, named, bounded, and swappable under a governed change.

**The cell is a pattern, not a package.** Draw it once; stamp it everywhere. That is the symmetry figure 0 insisted on: no region is the primary, none is a replica, and no box in the topology is labeled "headquarters." But stamping a pattern is not shipping one deployable unit — each component is stamped by its own accountable owner, inside its own administrative domain. The issuer plane in particular is a separate operator domain from the verification plane: separate storage, separate administration, separate audit readers, no shared infrastructure. An issuer that can read a verifier's records has quietly become the rendezvous point figure 2 spent a whole ceremony avoiding.

**What is inside, and who owns each part.** The *issuer plane* — Issuer operator — mints credentials and, on the per-use path, refills batches. The *verification plane* — regional verifier operator — runs the ceremony of figure 2. The *trust registry* — trust registry operator — owns metadata sync: provenance, version, expiry, rollback and conflict rejection, and the last-known-good snapshot the ceremony consults under the freshness policy FP-1. The *evidence and audit store* — regional platform operator — is the retention home for what the ceremony committed. The *supply chain data plane* — data plane owner — carries the goods-and-shipments reality that made this a supply chain rather than a wallet demo; its residency is governed by the external trade-compliance policy and its owner. That is what figure 1's dash meant: the ledger enumerates this class and delegates its governance. A dash is not permission to cross.

**Domains, and where the records live.** "Verifier domain" is a phrase figure 2 leaned on; deployment has to make it countable. A cell holds one *or more* verification-plane instances, each mapped one-to-one to an accountable verifier domain, and every checkpoint is assigned to exactly one domain. SU-1's at-most-once holds across everything inside a domain — which is also the honest reason not to sweep an airport, a customs post, and a warehouse into one plane just to widen the guarantee: that would make them one domain in name while handing them a shared surface to join on.

Each domain gets its own three: a transactional commit store, an independent failure journal, and an evidence partition, all keyed to the same domain identifier. The commit store is where figure 2's step 6 binds attempt identifier, unit state, outcome, and the evidence record in one transaction, and that record is the authoritative one — durable at commit time and readable there by the authorized regional operators and auditors of that domain, so no result ever outruns its own evidence. The audit store receives it by idempotent projection, owned, acknowledged, and retried; a projection that fails leaves failure evidence rather than silence. The failure journal writes to a separate sink that shares no datastore, availability, or failure domain with either store — a journal that dies with the thing it reports on cannot report it — carries the same domain-scoped viewer boundary, and if the journal's own state is unknown, the answer is no allow and a regional health record.

Rule 9's "never joined" is a property of this topology or it is nothing, so the prohibition covers more than a query API. No content-reading principal, interface, export, index, analytics job, or backup path spans two partitions, and platform administration does not confer cross-partition content read. Break-glass access, where a regulator requires it, is per-partition, time-limited, and audited. Merging domains or widening a reader set is a governed change that re-runs the rule 9 review, and a refused or failed cross-partition access leaves regional evidence for that domain's auditors.

**The adapter layer is the honest part.** Every real system has a place where the abstractions end and the invoices begin. Naming it is a design act; pretending it does not exist is how lock-in enters a diagram unannounced. So the adapter layer is drawn, and it is the only layer where a binding product name is load-bearing. It holds the binding categories as five separate kinds, so they are never confused with one another: data model, credential format, retrieval protocol, regulatory framework, cryptographic suite. The rest of the cell is procured too — an evidence store, a data plane, a registry stack, a transport — and those are named, substitutable components carrying interface obligations for residency, retention, and access control rather than binding semantics.

The gate lives here, run by the region's architecture and security owner, and it returns one of three verdicts, recorded as a ledger amendment rather than a ticket. *Qualified* — the binding meets figure 0's bar in full (fresh challenges bound to the verifier domain and transaction intent, anti-replay, no stable holder, device, credential, or status identifiers disclosed, minimized claims) and brings one of the qualifying privacy mechanisms: randomized unlinkable presentations, or per-use credentials issued in batches, a plain issuer-signed boolean qualifying only inside one of those. It is installed under the full promise. *Unqualified* — it is rejected, and no amount of adapter code substitutes, because an adapter can translate a format, not repair a correlator. *Reduced* — the only middle path figure 0 allows: a binding may be admitted with an explicitly approved reduction of the promise, its scope, its approver, and its ledger epoch recorded, and the cell's manifest carrying the reduced status where anyone can read it. What the gate never does is reduce a promise silently.

Because a swap is not a configuration change, the layer carries a versioned binding manifest, owned and published. Credentials may outlive deployments: reusable source credentials and unspent per-use batches minted under an outgoing binding stay in holders' custody, so a *planned* migration keeps the retiring binding installed for verification at least as long as the maximum lifetime of what was minted under it, with a reissuance path stated before the swap begins. An *emergency* retirement is the opposite reflex and needs saying separately: when a binding is compromised, it is disabled immediately, presentations under it resolve to deny — or to indeterminate with no allow where a deny would be wrong — and reissuance and holder notice run behind that, with the decision and its scope recorded as regional evidence for the affected domains' auditors. A migration window is a courtesy to holders; it is not a reason to keep a broken cryptographic suite alive.

The manifest is per cell, because regulatory frameworks and cryptographic suites are jurisdiction-specific by nature. Nothing here introduces remote presentation: a holder who travels may present, locally and in person, a credential issued under another cell's binding, and that local ceremony succeeds only where the destination cell's manifest supports the relevant binding. Outside that intersection the answer is indeterminate, not a silent failure. No inter-cell presentation route and no new federation cargo appear — figure 1's "remote cross-region presentation is not adopted" stands unchanged. The cells are identical in form and variant in bindings, and the manifest is where the variance is declared.

**What is not here.** There is no global control plane at runtime: no central registry serving decisions, no shared session store, no cross-region cache, no single pane of glass holding every region's evidence. Governance is a different question, and it has one shared role worth naming — the ledger's approval authority, which signs each epoch. Its key custody sits in a stated jurisdiction, and its failure behavior is stated too: if the authority is unavailable, epochs freeze and every cell keeps operating on its last-known-good snapshot under FP-1. That is a governance dependency with a declared blast radius, not a runtime dependency pretending to be absent.

The federation strip beneath the cells is a sync contract, and its two cargo behaviors differ. Trust anchors, revocation data, and policy activate on local validation alone — signed, versioned, unexpired, validated by the receiving trust registry under FP-1 — with no peer confirmation, because making fresh revocation wait on a peer's liveness would be a strange way to run a revocation system. Only the ledger's own epoch transition uses the staged path: distributed and validated under the current authority and schema, activated after both endpoints confirm the same epoch and content hash. No decision-time service runs on this strip. A deployment diagram is mostly an inventory of temptations declined.

**BL-1, specified and gated.** Figure 2 reserved the batch-refill residual to this figure's issuer plane, and here is its contract. Refills happen at a fixed cadence and quantity, with demand-driven emergency refills prohibited — but "fixed" only means something if it is written down first. So the issuer plane commits a signed, versioned *schedule manifest* in advance: scope, slot identifiers, cadence, quantity, tolerance window, effective window, and the authority allowed to change it. Every refill record references the manifest version and slot, with planned and actual time, quantity, and outcome. Refill records are event-level audit records in the issuing region — regional-only by figure 1's row, readable by that region's auditors, retained under figure 4's schedule.

The test then runs over those records alone, no ceremony trace required, and it matches in *both* directions: every scheduled slot has exactly one event — including a cover event in a window where nobody verified anything — and missing, extra, duplicate, out-of-tolerance, and quantity-mismatched events all count zero, with scheduled and observed counts equal. The two-way match is the whole point. A one-way check passes a schedule that quietly skips its zero-use weeks, and skipped weeks are exactly how consumption leaks. Schedule generation is itself checked for independence from consumption inputs, across traces that include zero-use windows, so a schedule computed from last quarter's demand and then published in advance does not slip through. A manifest published late, a manifest missing, or incomplete records mean the test cannot be run, and that is a failure, not a pass. The test re-runs every production window; a failure disables the per-use path again.

Failing quietly would be no better than not testing, so the verdict travels. The evaluator issues a coarse, signed, versioned, expiring *BL-1 verdict* — schedule window, pass, fail, or unknown, an evidence digest, and the issuing authority — and nothing else leaves: raw refill records stay in the issuing region. The verdict rides the federation interface as policy, a cargo class the ledger already enumerates, validated locally on arrival like any other policy. No verifier ever calls the issuer to ask. Each cell's binding manifest and enforcement point treat a verdict that is missing, expired, failed, or unknown as *per-use disabled* — and that covers batches already in holders' wallets, which is the case that matters, since an issuer who stops refilling does not thereby stop the credentials already out there. Falling back to derived presentations is available only where a qualifying derived binding is installed in that cell; where none is installed, the honest outcome is indeterminate and no allow — which is also why a cell that enables the per-use path should not be stamped without one.

**Opening and closing a cell.** A new cell is provisioned out-of-band by the ledger's approval authority together with the receiving region's trust registry operator: the current epoch and content hash — not the initial one — the schema, the authority keys, and the first anchor set, with an acceptance check that authenticates all of it before the cell serves a single ceremony. Closure is the same discipline in reverse, and it is easy to forget: a departing region must publish the revocation of its own anchors and collect peer confirmation *before* it stops, because otherwise its anchors stay valid in every peer's snapshot for up to FP-1's window and credentials it can no longer revoke keep verifying elsewhere. The closing region's trust registry operator runs it; the ledger's approval authority signs the closure epoch; the confirmation evidence is regional and readable by each peer's operators and auditors. If confirmation cannot be collected, the closure is not complete and the region does not stop.

From the closure epoch, credentials issued by that region resolve to indeterminate — with one clause that has to be explicit, because figure 0 lets a low-risk tier pass an indeterminate: this particular indeterminate has a ceiling of no allow, in every tier, or it is expressed as an outright deny by explicit revocation. A retired issuer whose credentials still open a low-risk gate is exactly the failure this paragraph exists to prevent. The closed cell's evidence store is disposed of under figure 4's retention, with its reader set named.
