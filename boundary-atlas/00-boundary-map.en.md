# The Boundary Atlas · 00 — Draw the borders before the arrows

*A design study in five figures. Vendor-neutral, model-neutral, deliberately generic — the point is a way of thinking, not a reference implementation. No client data appears anywhere in this series.*

**The brief — a composite, invented for this study.** A supply chain operating across regions that are drifting apart: data-residency laws, export controls, regulatory walls. Inside it, people and businesses have to prove who they are, constantly. The default failure mode is well known — every checkpoint collects a copy of everyone's documents, and the pile of copies becomes the largest liability in the system. The ask: verify the required identity claims in every region, keep raw PII and source documents out of checkpoints, and stay neutral about vendors and models.

When a brief like this asks for "a flow diagram," I don't start with flows.

**Why not flows first.** Both halves of the brief are boundary problems. Regional fragmentation is a question of jurisdiction: what may cross a border. Minimal-disclosure identity is a question of exposure: what may cross from a person to an institution. Draw sequences before those boundaries are fixed, and implementation convenience quietly starts making policy. So the first figure is not a flow — it is a map of borders and of the things allowed to cross them. Everything later in this series is derived from it.

![Figure 0 — boundary map: a holder above two symmetric regional cells, each with issuer, verifier, a regional trust registry holding a last-known-good snapshot, and supply-chain data plane; decision results return to the holder correlator-free; below the cells a thin federation interface syncs only trust anchors, revocation data, and policy; no arrow carries raw PII](00-boundary-map.en.svg)

**Reading the map.** The holder — a person or a business — retains custody of original documents and credentials. Regional cells are symmetric and self-sufficient: each has its own issuers (government and industry authorities), its own verifiers with local policy decision and enforcement points, its own supply-chain data plane — and a regional trust registry that owns metadata sync: it validates the provenance, version, and expiry of everything the federation interface delivers, rejects rollbacks and conflicts, and holds the last-known-good snapshot of trust anchors, revocation data, and policy — signed, versioned, expiring. Anything it cannot verify resolves to indeterminate. That registry is what "standalone" means in practice. Between cells runs a thin federation interface — a synchronization contract, not a runtime service — whose initial cargo list is short on purpose: trust anchors, revocation data, policy. It carries only items named in the approved border ledger — and amending that ledger is a governed change, not a config push.

**The cargo rule.** A flow, to me, is not an arrow. It is a declaration of cargo: what moves, across which border, in which direction, under what conditions. An arrow with no declared cargo leaves the boundary's most important governance questions unanswered. This map has exactly four cargo types — credentials (issuer → holder, within a region), predicate-style attestations (holder → verifier: an issuer-signed boolean such as "over 18 — true," or a zero-knowledge predicate proof where the binding supports one — never a birth date), decision results (verifier → holder: a coarse allow / deny / indeterminate with retry conditions, carrying no stable correlator), and federation metadata (cell ↔ cell).

The most load-bearing feature of the map, though, is an arrow that does not exist. No arrow carries raw PII or source documents. Not between cells, not into verifiers, not "temporarily, for caching." The absence is the design.

**Five invariants.** These are the decisions everything downstream must obey:

1. Raw PII and originals never leave the holder's hands or the issuer's own region. Verifiers receive attestations only — a predicate boolean or a proof, never the underlying attributes.
2. Every regional cell runs standalone. Partition is a premise, not an outage.
3. Only metadata enumerated in the border ledger (figure 1) is synchronized directly between regional cells through the federation interface — initially trust anchors, revocation data (group- or epoch-level status artifacts carrying no per-credential identifier), and policy.
4. Every component is named by role — issuer, holder, verifier, trust registry, policy decision and enforcement points. Standards and products are possible bindings, isolated in an adapter layer, and they are not one category: data models (W3C Verifiable Credentials), credential formats (SD-JWT), retrieval protocols (ISO mDL, device retrieval only), regulatory frameworks (eIDAS 2.0), cryptographic suites (BBS+). No load-bearing sentence in the design contains a product name.
5. Presentations are unlinkable at the credential layer. The transcripts and status evidence held by verifiers in different domains contain no stable holder or credential correlator. This is an admission gate for bindings, not something an adapter can bolt on afterward. Every binding must meet the common bar: fresh challenges bound to the verifier domain and transaction intent, anti-replay, no disclosure of stable holder, device, credential, or status identifiers, and a minimized claim set. On top of that bar it must bring a privacy mechanism — randomized, unlinkable presentations, or per-use credentials issued in batches; a plain issuer-signed boolean qualifies only inside one of those wrappers. A binding that cannot meet both is not adopted, or the promise is explicitly reduced.

**Scope and limits.** Invariant 5 closes one correlation channel — the credential itself. It does not close the others: time-and-place patterns, network and device metadata, biometrics at a checkpoint, business transaction identifiers. Those need their own controls, and the border ledger in figure 1 is where they get enumerated. A boundary map that claimed to solve all of them here would be lying with a diagram.

**What this buys.** A system that verifies without sending raw PII or source documents to checkpoints: the protocol artifacts a verifier holds provide no direct, stable holder or credential correlator. That closes one tracking channel — the credential itself — and only that one; the others are listed above and need their own controls. (That is what invariant 5 buys, and it is the hardest one to keep.) A supply chain that keeps operating through an inter-region partition, stated honestly: every checkpoint keeps running, but not every check accepts. Outcomes are three-valued — allow, deny, indeterminate. Stale or unverifiable status data yields indeterminate, and each risk tier decides what indeterminate means at its own checkpoint: a low-risk gate may pass, a high-assurance check refuses. Each decision also leaves its evidence where it happened: event-level audit records are regional-only, readable by that region's operators and auditors alone, and never cross the federation interface — figure 4 defines their schema, retention, and readers. And a design that survives procurement: bindings can be swapped; invariants cannot.

**Next.** Figure 1 is the least glamorous and most useful artifact in the series: the border ledger, a matrix of data classes × borders — including which predicate booleans issuers must commit to at issuance — the document you can put in front of legal and regulatory teams. Figure 2 zooms in on the verification ceremony itself, where a second arrow is deliberately missing: the verifier never calls the issuer back. Figure 4 rehearses partition — including what evidence an indeterminate or refused check leaves behind, and who is allowed to read it.
