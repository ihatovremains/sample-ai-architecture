# The Boundary Atlas · 01 — The border ledger

*A design study in five figures. Vendor-neutral, model-neutral, deliberately generic — the point is a way of thinking, not a reference implementation. No client data appears anywhere in this series.*

**Why the dull artifact matters.** Figure 0 fixed which boundaries exist and what may cross them. A promise like that is worth little while it lives only in a diagram: diagrams persuade, but they do not bind. The border ledger is the governable enumeration — data classes down the side, borders across the top, a verdict in every cell — and it becomes enforceable when each region compiles it into machine-evaluated policy at its decision points, applies it at its enforcement points, and validates the compilation with conformance and negative tests. It is the artifact you put in front of the governance owners — legal, compliance, data governance, architecture and security — not to inform them but to let them sign it. Architecture that governance cannot sign is architecture that ships with an asterisk.

**Reading the ledger.** The columns are four exchange paths from figure 0 — issue, present, result, federation sync — plus one residency constraint, a core regulatory question that reads the same for every row: may this data leave its origin region — the region where it was issued, generated, or observed? Custody is not a boundary; it is a condition. A credential leaves its origin region only in the holder's custody (△¹); an unlinkable presentation never leaves it at all (✕⁶). Presentation is local by design: the holder presents where they stand, and an unlinkable presentation is derived by the holder, during the local ceremony, from a source credential — remote cross-region presentation is not adopted, and adopting it would be a ledger amendment that reopens the presentation-capable leaf rows and their residency rules. The rows are data classes of two kinds. Identity artifacts are classified by a versioned discriminator into exactly one row — that taxonomy has no overlaps. Content classes are the opposite: they are evaluated field by field across every row a payload touches, the most restrictive verdict wins, and wrapping a forbidden class inside a permitted container is itself a ledger violation, checked against versioned schemas at the federation interface. Three crossing verdicts exist — may cross (✓), never crosses (✕), crosses only under a named condition (△) — plus two honest states: a dash (—) for classes governed outside this ledger, and obs for data not declared as cargo but observed by the receiving checkpoint, constrained under its own numbered rule. Each condition has a numbered rule ID that amendments can cite. A blank cell is not a decision; this ledger has no blank cells.

**The identity rows are a taxonomy, not a pile.** Figure 0's "attestation" is the umbrella for the approved identity presentation cargo a verifier receives; the ledger needs sharper knives. So identity artifacts are classified by the versioned discriminator into exactly one of three leaves: a reusable source credential (issued once, lives in the holder's wallet, never presented), a qualified per-use credential (batch-issued under figure 0's binding gate, cryptographically distinct per instance, intended for a single presentation), and a qualified unlinkable presentation (derived by the holder, during the local ceremony, from a source credential). One artifact, one leaf — the discriminator decides, and the most restrictive rule then applies to embedded content, not to the leaf choice: raw PII or a biometric template inside any artifact still drags in the stricter row. Every presentation-capable leaf carries the gate's numbered rule; the reusable source credential still never crosses the Present lane. Single use is a reserved control, not a settled one. The reservation SU-1 fixes the interface now and leaves the sequence to figure 2: a presentation attempt triggers an idempotent consumption state transition with exactly one commit point, and until that commit is confirmed — response loss and retries included — the PEP must not allow that presentation; indeterminate is the ceiling, never allow. The regional verifier operator is accountable for SU-1 end to end; figure 4 supplies the durable decision evidence — regional-only, readable by regional operators and auditors, never crossing the federation interface. SU-1 closes only when its test passes: first use, controlled retry, replay and reuse rejection, unknown state yields no allow, and evidence stays with its authorized readers.

No system lane carries raw PII, source documents, or biometric reference data. Their only permitted movement in this design is in the holder's custody: the holder never presents them to a checkpoint, and no checkpoint may copy or retain them. Biometric references are enrolled and matched on the holder's device; an issuer-supplied template would trigger both the biometric row and the credential row, and the stricter verdict wins. Event-level audit records remain inside the regional audit boundary: regional operators and auditors read them where they were written. Centralizing event-level audit trails across regions would create a surveillance channel with better branding.

Checkpoint times, locations, and network metadata are a different kind of row. They are not declared attestation cargo — the receiving checkpoint observes or derives them from the transport the moment a presentation happens, and a ledger that pretended observation away would be lying with a table. So observation gets its own verdict (obs) and its own constraints, written as schema and retention controls: regional-only, kept per verifier, purpose-limited, no stable subject key retained, never joined across verifiers — the regional platform operator owns that prohibition, and figure 4 defines schema and retention. Ambient biometric capture at checkpoints is prohibited outright; the only biometrics in this design live on the holder's device.

The △ and — cells are where the honesty lives. Operational telemetry has no permitted crossing in the initial ledger: admitting a metric is a named amendment carrying the metric, its field schema, purpose, aggregation window, cohort threshold, suppression controls, retention, and an accountable owner — because a cohort floor alone is not a privacy guarantee; aggregates still leak without formal controls, so the amendment must name its privacy mechanism, not just its cohort size. Business and trade data gets the dash: it is governed outside this ledger, by an external trade-compliance policy under that policy's owner — and naming what you do not govern is as load-bearing as governing it.

**The predicate commitment register.** Bindings that use issuer-signed booleans force a governance decision figure 0 could only gesture at: every predicate must be enumerated at issuance. "Over 18," "sanctions-clear," "licensed importer" — each is a commitment the issuer signs up to, and the initial register appears below the ledger with one entry filled end to end: age_over_18 — meaning age ≥ 18 at presentation time — with its issuing authority, legal jurisdiction, version, freshness bound, and purpose. The rest sit as inactive candidates, and an inactive candidate crosses nothing. Each entry is a small contract, not just a name. Adding or activating one is a ledger amendment with governance review, not a sprint task. Bindings that carry zero-knowledge predicate proofs relax the enumeration, not the register: the allowed predicate *classes* still live here — issuer profiles constrain the schema and the committed values, and the verifier's policy decision point controls, through the synced policy, which predicate classes it will request or accept.

**The correlation-channel register.** Figure 0's scope-and-limits paragraph listed what invariant 5 does not close: time-and-place patterns, network and device metadata, biometrics at the checkpoint, business transaction identifiers. Each of those channels now has a row in the matrix, and the register below the ledger names an accountable owner for each. This is where "out of scope" stops meaning "unowned."

**How it stays alive.** The ledger is policy — and policy is already a cargo class. So the ledger itself synchronizes to every regional cell through the same federation interface it governs, signed and versioned like everything else the regional trust registry validates. It has one canonical signer — its approval authority — and every release is identified by epoch plus content hash. Across a partition, effective policy is computed, not negotiated: the intersection of the two endpoints' allowlists, with conditions conjoined. Anything unknown, missing, or same-epoch-with-a-different-hash resolves to indeterminate — an equivocating ledger is a conflict, not a tie-break — and conditional classes cross only when both endpoints confirm the same epoch. The ledger's own update is the one carefully shaped exception, or epoch N+1 could never reach a peer still on N: a candidate ledger is distributed, validated, and staged under the currently active authority and schema, activates only after both endpoints confirm the same epoch and content hash, and ordinary conditional cargo resumes on the new epoch after that confirmation. One more honesty clause: the initial ledger, its schema, and the authority keys are provisioned out-of-band together with the first trust anchors. Every chain needs a first link that does not arrive through the pipe it validates.
